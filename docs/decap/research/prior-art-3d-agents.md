> Does anything combine a 3D chip view, live editing, live simulation and agents aimed at parts of a real chip?
> Generated by Yuyao's Claude on Sat 7 Nov 2026 with a multi-agent workflow (6 component sweeps, 2 adversarial closest-match hunts). Treat as research notes: sources are linked inline; vendor numbers are self-reported.

# Is there already a 3D chip visualiser with live editing, simulation and AI agents?

## 1. Direct answer

No. As far as we could find, nothing does all five. The closest is **Tiny Tapeout Explorer** by Alexander Mordvintsev ([znah.net/tiny_explorer](https://znah.net/tiny_explorer/)). It shows real Tiny Tapeout chips in 3D in your browser and simulates them. While it runs, each wire in the 3D layout changes colour by its logic state. That covers 3 of the 5 (A, C, E). It has **no editing and no AI**. A handful of small, newer tools let an AI look at one region of a real layout. All of them are 2D or explain-only, and none of them simulate.

## 3. Which capability is the rarest?

**D, an agent you aim at a part of a visual chip view, is the newest and rarest.** The tools closest to it:
- **GDS-3D-VIEWER:** the only 3D viewer with AI aimed at a selection. It gives a one-off explanation of the selected cell or shape. It cannot act, simulate or edit, and has 0 stars.
- **PhotonForge LiveViewer:** an agent can move around a real SKY130 die (Caravel), from the whole chip down to one device. It is 2D, look-only, commercial, and has no simulation.
- **klayout-mcp:** an agent can query and render one region of a real layout. It is read-only and 2D.
- **Z80 Explorer:** the only tool where an AI agent can probe a real chip *while it is being simulated*. But the chip is a 1970s Z80, the view is 2D, and the design is fixed.

The big vendors' agents are task-based ("fix this RTL", "close timing"). They are not "click this block", and none shows a visual chip view.

**C on the real physical layout** is rare, but it has been done. Tiny Tapeout Explorer does it for modern chips. Visual 6502 and Z80 Explorer do it for vintage ones.

**B against the layout** is missing for a different reason: it is barely possible. Going from changed RTL to a new layout means synthesis plus place-and-route, which takes minutes even for a tiny Tiny Tapeout tile. So "real-time editing" of a real layout is not a gap someone forgot to fill. Realistic versions are: edit RTL and re-simulate at gate level; force or override a signal on the existing layout; or show a "what would change" diff.

## 4. Does "nobody combines all five" make it a winning project?

Not by itself. The risks:

- **It reads as a feature list.** "3D + editing + sim + agents + real chips" is five checkboxes. Judges will ask "what problem does this solve?" and "who needs this?" A combination nobody has built is not the same as a need nobody has met.
- **It is too big for a weekend.** Tiny Tapeout Explorer alone is a C++/WASM layout parser, a circuit extractor and a switch-level simulator, built over months. Rebuilding A+C+E in a hackathon would eat all your time. The good news: it is Apache-2.0, so you can build *on top of it* and spend your effort on D.
- **Someone else could do it.** Because Tiny Tapeout Explorer is open source, anyone could fork it and add an MCP server. Z80 Explorer already shows that pattern working. The vendors (Cadence, Synopsys, Siemens) are shipping agents through 2026, but theirs are closed, licence-gated, not visual, and aimed at professional teams. They are not a threat in a student or Tiny Tapeout setting. They do show that "agents for chips" alone is no longer new.
- **Some claims are thin.** Chipmind, Cognichip and the vendor agent claims are marketing only. Our search budget also ran out before we could check Hackaday or the DAC 2026 demo floor. Treat "nobody has it" as "we didn't find it", not proof.

**The one job this combination would do better than anything else: understanding or debugging a chip you didn't design.** Picture it: you point at a block on a real taped-out chip, an agent tells you what it is, and then it *proves* it by running the chip live and lighting up the wires it's talking about. Today that takes four separate tools and a lot of expertise: a GDS viewer, a netlist, a simulator and a waveform viewer.

Who would use it:
- Tiny Tapeout participants and reviewers trying to read other people's tiles (each shuttle has hundreds of designs on one chip)
- students learning how real silicon works
- teachers
- hardware security and reverse-engineering people, who today use 2D tools like [HAL](https://github.com/emsec/hal)

**Suggested pitch:** "Click any part of a real chip. An AI explains what it does and shows you, live, on the silicon." Treat B as a small extra ("force this signal and watch what happens"), not full redesign. Make D plus C on a real layout the headline, because that pairing is what nobody has shipped. Demo it on a Tiny Tapeout design you can actually hold in your hand.
